## The Problem

Most professionals chase every new release, skim blog posts, and stack up half-finished books and courses. They mistake volume for learning. I personally did this for a few years before realizing a common pattern: a small set of books kept paying off long time after I read them.

This post is about the small foundational set. Roughly 25 books across software engineering, math, decision-making, and systems thinking. I also explain why investing deeply in fewer texts beats skimming broadly across many.


<a id="orgf9d0b96"></a>

## Why Foundational Learning Works

Three mental models explain why depth beats breadth here

**The Pareto Principle:** A small fraction of books contain a disproportionate share of the knowledge we actually use at work. Not all reading is equal. Most of my practical engineering knowledge traces back to less than 20% of what I've read.

**The Lindy Effect:** A book that has been relevant for 20 years is likely to remain relevant for another 20. As an example consider Shakespeare's works. They have been there for centuries and will surely be there for a few more. Foundational material has a long half-life — the time it takes for half the knowledge to become
outdated. Frameworks and tutorials go stale in few years, fundamentals won't.

**Compounding:** Foundational knowledge doesn't just persist, they accumulate. Understanding data structures deeply doesn't just help once; it compounds every time you design a system, debug a performance issue, or evaluate a new tool. You build on top of it instead of re-learning them from scratch.

The practical consequence: time spent on a very few foundational books builds more skill and helps make better decisions over a career than the same time spread across dozens of surface-level reads.

## What I Rediscovered

After more than two decades of reading, I reached the following conclusion

-   Pareto Principle:  Roughly 25 books provided the large majority of the skill and knowledge I actually apply at work and in making better decisions

-   Lindy Effect: The oldest books on this list K&R, SICP, GEB are still the most useful. Even though they were written 30-40 years ago they are still relevant

-   Compounding: I don't have to re-learn these topics from scratch. Every new problem I encounter builds on top of what these books built. That's the real return on learning the fundamentals, not just the first, but every subsequent year where the knowledge keeps working.

Start with these if your interests overlap with mine. If it doesn't, apply the same filter to your own domain. Identify the few valuable texts in your domain that have long half-lives and deep explanatory power. Focus your time and energy on reading those properly.

Improve quality instead of chasing quantity. Depth in foundational knowledge compounds and yields great value.
